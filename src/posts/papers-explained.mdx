## Why this series

The direct trigger for this series came in September and October 2026.
In September, OpenAI announced that an AI system had solved the Navier–Stokes problem, one of the most famous open problems in mathematics.
Then, on October 6, it released [722 math manuscripts in one day](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/10/08/openai-withdraws-preprints-722-manuscripts-unsolved-math-problems/).
By its own count, about half of the results were not yet checked, and a sign error made it withdraw three of them the next day.

Many mathematicians were angry.
The Association for Human Mathematics asked mathematicians to stop working with OpenAI.
Terence Tao posted [its statement](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/07/ahm-statement-on-openais-october-6-release-of-mathematical-documents/) on his blog.

I understand the anger.
No person can read 722 papers in a day.
A result that nobody checks or understands adds little to mathematics.

A few months earlier, in July 2026, Tao gave a lecture at the International Congress of Mathematicians called [Mathematics in the age of AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16753).
His point, roughly: AI tools are getting good at producing proofs, but a proof that no one understands is not finished.
As machines take on more of the work, mathematicians should put more effort into explanation, so that results are actually understood.

I agree, and it made me look at my own field honestly.
We mathematicians were not doing a good job of this even before AI.
Ask a mathematician friend whether they understand the work of a colleague in the next field over.
Often the honest answer is "not really".
For everyone outside mathematics, the gap is far wider.

My own papers are a good example.
From 2014 to 2022 I did research in math and scientific computing as my job, and that work ended up as a handful of journal papers.
I still do research today, as a hobby.
Very few people will ever read those papers.
They are written for a small group of experts, and they skip every step that those experts already know.
For anyone else, the interesting idea in each paper hides behind pages of notation.

I think most of those ideas are simple at heart, and many are fun.
A computer can find the outline of a shape by blurring it.
A laser can make a gas hum, and a tuning fork can listen for it.
You can simulate an infinite space on a finite computer, with no error from the cut.

Still, I do not think the answer is to boycott AI.
The answer is to use it with a clear goal: more understanding, not just more output.
Used that way, AI can help close the gap between what is known and what is understood, and I think the path forward can be much brighter.
This series is my small experiment in doing that.
It tells the story of each paper, one at a time, in plain language.

## Who it is for

I write for a curious reader who has taken calculus and a little linear algebra.
Think of a second-year undergrad in science or engineering.
You do not need to know any physics beyond high school, or any numerical methods.

If you are an expert, you can still enjoy the series.
Each post links to the paper and the code, so you can check the details.

## What to expect

Each post explains one paper and follows the same plan:

1. **A hook:** something you have seen, such as a photo or a drop of oil.
2. **The question:** what we wanted to compute, in one sentence.
3. **Why it's hard:** what goes wrong with the obvious approach.
4. **The key idea:** one idea, with one picture.
5. **Behind the scenes:** what we tried first and what surprised us.
6. **Go further:** the paper, the code and related posts.

A post takes about ten minutes to read.
Formulas appear only after a picture, and each one comes with a sentence that says what it means.
Heavier math goes into "For the curious" boxes. You can skip them, and the post still makes sense.
Where it is cheap and fun, a post includes a small demo that runs in your browser.

The first post explains [how to find the outline of a shape by blurring it](/posts/image-segmentation).
Next, I will go through my other papers, one at a time.
They cover water in tiny channels, oil and vinegar in a jar, lasers that make a gas hum, and the math inside large simulations.

That is a starting point, not a fixed plan or a limit.
I start with my own work because I know it best.
Later, I may explain other people's work that I find interesting.
That work may not even be a paper.
One day, research may stop using papers as its main way to share new results, and this series can follow it there.

New posts appear every few weeks. You can follow them with the [RSS feed](/feed.xml) or on the [series page](/series/papers-explained).

## How it is made

I write this series with help from AI assistants.
Today I use Claude. Later I may add other AI tools, or change tools as they improve.

The assistant reads the public version of each paper, usually on arXiv, and drafts the post.
It also writes the code for the figures and the demos.
The figures are new: the code reruns each method on made-up test data, so nothing is copied from a journal.

My part is to check the science, fix what the draft gets wrong, and add the story that only I can tell.
Every post ends with an "AI use disclosure" that says which tools helped with that post and exactly who did what.
Tao's lecture asks for this kind of open disclosure too.

Without this help, these posts would stay on my "some day" list.
With it, I can turn years of research into something people may actually read.

## Why you might care

The methods in these papers are not just for experts.
They sit inside tools that you use or will use: photo editors, gas sensors and engineering simulations.

The ideas also travel well.
"Measure a length with a blur" and "solve on the boundary, not the whole room" are tricks you can use in other problems.
And each post shows how a research question goes from a vague wish to an algorithm with a proof.
That path is the same in most of science and engineering, and it is rarely shown in plain language.

If a post is unclear, or if it makes you curious about something, I would like to [hear about it](/contact).
